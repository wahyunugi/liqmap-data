Principles · what the data has shown so far

Tested on Binance data for 8 coins, 2020 to Nov 2026 · last reviewed 22 Nov 2026 (buying the pullback after a bounce, signs of the last low, no certainty about the last low, what comes after the bounce, bounce map, very heavy volume, shorting fresh lows and the 200-day average added; all 9 experiments from the literature added: strength against dips, big moves cluster, futures premium, time of day, volume and order flow, ETF flow streaks, exchange balances, public attention, big liquidation days)

Zones: accounts under 2, 2 to 3, above 3; top traders under 1.2, 1.2 to 1.9, above 1.9. Past results in each zone from 336 lows and 589 highs on 8 coins (Dec 2021 to Nov 2026); average over all zones: lows 49%, highs 35%. The two ratios together were checked by fitting on seven coins and testing on the eighth, in turn (score 0.65 for lows and 0.62 for highs, where 0.5 is useless and 1 is perfect), so this shifts the odds, it does not decide them. The top-trader figure comes from Binance's daily archive and can be a day old. Longs in loss: the estimated share of open Binance contracts that were opened above the current price, worked out from hourly changes in open interest (see principle 8); when it was above 95%, lows worked less often (37% against 49%), but that gap is not statistically firm.
  1. Watch who is already long, small accounts and big ones. At 411 lows across 8 coins, the more Binance accounts were long, the less often the low worked: under 1.5 long accounts per short account 69% won, 1.5 to 2.0 56%, 2.0 to 2.5 49%, 2.5 to 3.0 45%, 3.0 to 4.0 41%, above 4.0 37%. The top-trader ratio (the largest accounts, by position size) is the stronger signal: lowest two fifths (ratio under about 1.2) won 62% to 69%, the highest fifth (above about 1.9) won 28%. For a long entry, wait for both to come down.
  2. The same ratios warn at highs and set the direction of big moves. At 589 highs across 8 coins, a real top followed 51% of the time when the top-trader ratio was in its highest fifth (above about 2.45), against 23% in the lowest fifth. Of 1,672 big moves (3 times the normal 6-hour swing, at least a day apart, 8 coins), 66% were up when the account ratio was under 1.5, 56% at 2.0 to 2.5, and 37% when it was above 4.0. Same direction in 2020 to 2023 and 2024 to 2026. BTC is the most extreme: with the ratio at 3 or more, only 3 of its 14 big moves were up. The "Big-move risk" panel below shows where BTC and ETH stand now.
  3. ETH is BTC with extra downside. Daily correlation is 0.82 (0.91 in 2026). On days BTC fell 5% or more, ETH fell more 71% of the time. On days BTC rose 5% or more, ETH rose more only 39% of the time. They bottom together (26 of 33 BTC bottoms had an ETH bottom within 3 days) and neither leads the other by an hour. Read BTC's signals even for an ETH trade.
  4. Real money confirms, it does not lead. Spot traders and ETFs were still selling at nearly every real bottom. ETF inflows came in the week after: 63% of reversals against 29% of failed lows. Do not wait for inflows before a bottom, but treat a low as suspect if they do not come after it.
  5. Big moves go away from the bigger untouched liquidation pile, most of all when the crowd is long. Of 497 big moves in BTC and ETH since 2020, 42% went up when more short liquidations were stacked within 2% above price than long liquidations within 2% below, against 59% the other way round. With the account ratio also above 2.5, only 8 of 53 went up (15%). For ordinary moves the liquidation map shows nothing (50/50).
  6. Fed decisions and US inflation reports shake the hour, not the day, and the direction is not predictable. In the hours of a Fed rate decision, a big hourly move in BTC was about 5 times more likely than in the same hours on other weekdays (11.6% against 2.2%). In the hours of the monthly US inflation report (CPI, the consumer price index) it was 3 times more likely (11.4% against 3.8%). The monthly jobs report barely mattered (5.3% against 4.1%). The full day was no more likely to be a big day (about 16% either way), and the following week was up no more often than usual. Tested on 56 Fed decisions, 82 inflation reports and 82 jobs reports since 2020.
  7. The 4-year cycle keeps its timing but shrinks in size. The last three tops came 526 to 548 days after the halving. Gains and crashes got smaller each cycle. Only 3 to 4 cycles exist, so this is weak evidence.
  8. Positions in loss ("underwater") are one number, and it adds little beyond the crowd ratio. Every futures contract has a long and a short opened at the same price, so the share of longs in loss always equals the share of shorts in profit: there are not four separate groups, only one number plus the question of who is long (the ratios above). Binance does not publish entry prices, so the share is estimated from hourly changes in open interest since 2020. At lows where more than 95% of longs were in loss, the low worked 37% of the time (91 cases on 8 coins) against 49% otherwise, in the same direction in each of three periods. But those cases bunch into few weeks and a gap that size could be chance (about 1 in 4). At highs, the share of shorts in loss made no difference (33% against 40%). For the direction of big moves it carries the same information as the account ratio and adds nothing once that is known. How far open interest had already fallen from its 30-day peak ("the weak hands have given up") did not matter either. Real, not estimated, figures exist only on Hyperliquid: see "Share of size in loss" in its panel.
  9. A short squeeze needs shorts as fuel, and the only tested sign of that fuel is a low crowd ratio. The usual checklist for a squeeze is: funding clearly negative, open positions rising while price is flat or falling, and few accounts long. Tested on BTC and ETH, one reading per day since 2020, with "worked" meaning price rose one normal weekly swing before it fell one, within 14 days (52% on an average day). Account ratio under 1.5: 58% (356 separate weeks); above 2.5: 41% (201 weeks). Funding clearly negative (below -0.005% per 8 hours): 46% (50 weeks, nearly all in 2020 to 2022), so no help on its own. Open positions up more than 5% in a week while price did not rise: 53% overall, but 66% in 2023 to 2024 and 41% in 2025 to 2026, so not reliable. Negative funding together with rising positions looked strong (price 7 days later +4.8% in the middle case) but happened in only 16 weeks, almost all before 2023: too few to trust. So for a squeeze, read the crowd ratio first and treat the other two as supporting detail, not as signals. A later test (principle 12) adds one detail: funding only slightly below zero (between -0.005% and 0) was followed by fewer crashes than usual (16% against 28%), while clearly negative funding was not (24%).
  10. Do not buy a fresh low; strength is the better-tested entry. On 8 coins since 2020, entering long the first hour price broke its 20-day high won 56% of 991 entries, entering the first hour it broke its 20-day low won 46% of 795, and a random entry won 51% ("won" = price rose one normal weekly swing before it fell one, within 30 days). The 10-point gap between strength and dips is unlikely to be chance (about 1 in 60, tested by shuffling whole weeks) and pointed the same way in all 8 coins. By period: 65% against 51% in 2020 to 2021, 49% against 51% in 2022 to 2023 (no difference in that bear market), 53% against 41% in 2024 to 2026. Buying a fresh 20-day low at once was never better than a random entry. Dips do bounce the next day more often (57% of days up against 50%), but the gain did not last 7 to 30 days. Strength wins through a few large runs, not a higher hit rate: the day after a 20-day high was up only 49% of the time. Idea from published research (Quantpedia, 2015 to 2024 data), confirmed here on other coins and a later period.
  11. Big moves come in clusters: the day after one is the riskiest day. On 8 coins since 2020 (one reading per coin per day), a big move started within the next 24 hours on 13.0% of all days. Within a day after a big move ended it was 28.7% (2,602 days); after 1 to 3 days 14.0%; 3 to 7 days 11.6%; 7 to 14 days 7.9%; more than 14 days 9.8%. The "within a day" figure was 29%, 30% and 28% in 2020 to 2021, 2022 to 2023 and 2024 to 2026, and between 22% and 34% in each of the 8 coins, so chance is ruled out. Direction of the follow-up (when it came within 3 days): after a big move up the next was up again 61% of the time (631 cases); after a big move down the next was down again only 52% (532 cases), a coin flip. "Calm before the storm" did not hold: with the 7-day price range at its narrowest a big move followed on 10% of days, at its widest on 24%. In practice: no tight stops and no high leverage in the day after a big move. Principle 14 found that trading volume measures the same clustering more precisely, so the "Big-move risk" panel uses volume. A big move is a 6-hour change of at least 3 times the normal 6-hour swing; the bar rises after volatile days, so this test is conservative.
  12. High funding is not a crash warning any more; the futures premium is a mild one, and a low premium is the more useful side. Published research (BIS, "Crypto carry") found that futures trading far above spot predicts crashes. Tested on BTC and ETH, one reading per day since 2020, with a "crash" meaning a fall of at least 2 normal weekly swings at some point in the next 30 days (28% of average days). High funding (0.015% to 0.03% per 8 hours): 30% overall, but 40% in 2020 to 2021 and 12% in 2024 to 2026, so it worked in the period the paper studied and not since. Very high funding (above 0.03%): 33% in both periods against 23% and 30%. Futures premium over spot in its top 10% (7-day average above +0.079%): 40%, with the price a month later 3.1% lower in the middle case; 37% in 2020 to 2021 and 36% in 2024 to 2026 (only 13 weeks). Premium in its bottom 10% (below -0.050%, futures cheaper than spot): 18%, at 0% in 2020 to 2021 and 17% in 2024 to 2026, but no help in the 2022 to 2023 bear market (24% against 29%). One pattern looks strong but rests on about five episodes (March 2020, April to May 2021, November to December 2021, April 2024): high funding while price had already fallen over the previous 30 days was followed by a crash 71% of the time (86 days). Treat that as a description of those crashes, not a tested rule.
  13. Time of day: big moves start in the early evening WIB; one small early-morning drift is real; nothing else is stable. Tested on 8 coins, hourly since 2020, in three periods. When big moves start (2024 to 2026): in 0.6% to 1.0% of hours between 08:00 and 14:00 WIB, against 3.3% to 3.6% between 18:00 and 21:00 WIB, around the US market open (average hour 1.8%). In 2020 to early 2022 the hours were nearly flat (1.4% to 2.4%), so this concentration is recent. Weekends are calmer: 1.4% of hours against 2.0% on weekdays. The window named in published research, 04:00 to 06:00 WIB (21:00 to 23:00 UTC; Quantpedia, 2015 to early 2022), still showed a gain for BTC after that sample ended: +0.032% a day in March 2022 to 2023 (could be chance) and +0.061% a day in 2024 to 2026 (not chance), when the other 22 hours together gave +0.010%. That is smaller than the cost of a round trip, and only 52% to 55% of days were up, so it can time an entry already wanted but is not a trade by itself. No other hour, session or weekday held: the 24-hour pattern of one period was unrelated to the next, and US hours went from the strongest session in 2020 to 2022 to slightly negative since.
  14. Heavy trading volume is the best warning of a big move; order flow adds only a little on direction. On 8 coins since 2020, one reading per coin per day: with futures volume over the last 24 hours below 0.6 times its 30-day norm, a big move started within the next 24 hours on 6.0% of days; 0.6 to 0.8 times, 7.7%; 0.8 to 1.0, 10.0%; 1.0 to 1.3, 12.1%; 1.3 to 1.8, 17.5%; above 1.8 times, 30.2% (2,312 days). The top band was 28%, 29% and 33% in 2020 to 2021, 2022 to 2023 and 2024 to 2026. Once volume is known, "a big move ended in the last day" (principle 11) and unusually steady buy and sell flow (the early-warning signal in published research, arXiv 2607.27070) add nothing: both mostly reflect heavy volume. Fitted on 2020 to 2023 and scored on 2024 to 2026, volume alone did as well as all of them together. Direction: when price fell over the previous 24 hours and aggressive selling (orders that hit the price on offer) clearly outweighed buying, the next big move was up only 43% of the time (850 cases; 48%, 45%, 39% in the three periods), against 50% when price fell without heavy selling and 62% after price rose. On ordinary days order flow said nothing about the next 24 hours (49% to 53% up).
  15. ETF flows follow price; outflow streaks do not predict further falls. Daily flows of the US spot ETFs (BTC since January 2024, ETH since July 2024), each treated as known only at 13:00 WIB the next day. A day's flow moved with the price change of the 5 days before it (correlation +0.56 for BTC, +0.45 for ETH) and not with the 5 days after (+0.03 and +0.01), in both halves of the sample: money arrives after price rises and leaves after it falls. After 5 straight outflow days BTC was up 7 days later 29% of the time and after 7 straight days 80% (17 and 10 cases; opposite directions, so noise); 30 days later 59% and 60%, against 54% on any day. The first inflow day after an outflow streak of 5 days or more was not a buy signal: BTC rose over the next 30 days in 9 of 16 cases, ETH in 6 of 12. One weak hint: for ETH, a 10-day outflow in its largest fifth was followed by a rise over 30 days only 27% of the time, but most of that is the price fall that caused the outflow; compared only with days after a similar fall, it was 32% against 42% (BTC 46% against 56%), on small, overlapping samples. Published research found a small lead of flows over price in 2024 (FalconX); in this longer sample it does not hold.
  16. Exchange balances do not predict price, despite the textbook story. Daily balances in the published wallets of 10 large exchanges since December 2022 (DefiLlama; a one-day jump of more than 15% at one exchange is treated as a wallet-list change and removed). The net flow over 7 days, read at 13:00 WIB, against the price over the next 7 days: stablecoins arriving and BTC +0.06 (correlation), stablecoins and ETH +0.08, BTC arriving and BTC -0.08, ETH arriving and ETH -0.07. All are weak and most changed size or sign between 2023 to 2024 and 2024 to 2026. The textbook reading (coins sent to exchanges to be sold, withdrawn to be held) held once: in 2023 to 2024 BTC rose over the next week 64% of the time after the biggest withdrawals and 47% after the biggest deposits; in 2024 to 2026, 44% and 45%. Stablecoins leaving exchanges was a mild warning only in one period per coin. Published research found flows predict price 1 to 6 hours ahead (arXiv 2411.06327); at a daily horizon nothing is left (next-day correlation 0.00 to 0.04). The panel below shows the balances for context, not as a signal.
  17. Public attention used to predict price and no longer does. Published research (Liu and Tsyvinski, Review of Financial Studies 2021) found that a jump in Google searches predicts higher crypto prices over the following weeks. Tested with a free stand-in, daily readers of the English Wikipedia articles "Bitcoin" (since 2015) and "Ethereum" (since 2018), against daily prices from Bitstamp and Binance, one reading per week, each figure treated as known only at the end of the next day. Readers in the last week against the median of the past year, then the price change over the next 30 days (correlation): BTC +0.25 in 2015 to 2019, +0.21 in 2020 to 2022, +0.09 in 2023 to 2026; ETH +0.29, +0.24, -0.06. Until 2022 it held even after removing the previous month's price move, and BTC was higher 30 days later 60% to 68% of the time when attention was in its top fifth. Since 2023 it has gone, and for ETH it turned around (higher a month later only 33% of the time in the top fifth). A likely reason, not proven: Wikipedia readership has shrunk (BTC article: 41,000 readers a day in 2017, 3,600 in 2026), so it no longer measures the crowd. A jump in readers came before slightly larger price swings (+0.12 to +0.25 for BTC), which trading volume (principle 14) already captures better. The Wikipedia line on the chart is context only.
  18. A big liquidation day is not a bounce signal on its own: it bounced in the rising market and kept falling in the falling one. Daily Binance liquidations of BTC and ETH (Coinalyze, December 2022 to November 2026); a big day has at least 4 times the median of the previous 90 days. Compared fairly, with days when price fell just as hard (more than 1.5 normal daily swings) but liquidations were ordinary: after a big drop with big long liquidations price was higher 7 days later 58% of the time (69 days, 35 separate episodes), against 43% after a big drop with ordinary liquidations. But that came from one phase: 79% in 2022 to 2024 (38 days) and 32% in 2025 to 2026 (31 days, including January and May 2026), against 40% and 45% for ordinary drops. Allowing for the size of the drop, the extra effect of liquidations is within what chance produces. So a forced flush clears the way up in a rising market and is just another leg down in a falling one; the crowd ratio (principle 1) is what tells them apart. The hour after the heaviest 2% of liquidation hours did not bounce either: up 44% for BTC and 38% for ETH (about 25 cases each, September to November 2026, too few for a verdict; hourly liquidations are now collected, so this can be repeated in 2027). Idea from published research (arXiv 2608.21888), which named liquidation-driven moves as an open question.
  19. Waiting for price to be back above its 200-day average buys confirmation at a high price, and helps BTC more than ETH. Daily prices since 2014 (BTC) and 2018 (ETH). Over the next 90 days, BTC rose by +12.5% in the middle case from days above its 200-day average and fell by 2.0% from days below; for ETH the gap was small (+5.5% against +4.3%). The cost: the first close back above the average after a long stretch below came when price was already 18% to 82% above the preceding low for BTC and 42% to 99% for ETH, and it was not always right (ETH in April 2022 fell 70% in the following 90 days). The same is true of the other confirmations: a break above the 20-day high (principle 10) and money flowing back in (principles 4 and 15) both come after price has moved. Lows themselves are close to a coin flip unless the crowd has cleared out (principle 1), so the only tested way to buy near a low is to wait for the crowd ratio, not for price. Method: research/ma200_entry.py.
  20. Shorting a fresh low works modestly, and better when the crowd is still long. The mirror of principle 10: going short the first hour price broke its 20-day low (8 coins since 2020, entries at least 3 days apart), "won" if price fell one normal weekly swing before it rose one, within 30 days. Overall 54% of 850; 49% in 2020 to 2021, 49% in 2022 to 2023 and 59% in 2024 to 2026 (BTC and ETH alone in 2024 to 2026: 60% of 101). By the Binance account ratio at entry: under 2, 44%; 2 to 2.5, 51%; 2.5 to 3, 55%; 3 or more, 60% (62% in 2024 to 2026). So the edge exists only in the recent period and when most accounts are still long, and even then about 4 in 10 such shorts were stopped out first. A one-swing stop is wide (about 6% for BTC and 8.5% for ETH in November 2026), so leverage decides how much of the margin that costs. Method: research/short_fresh_lows.py.
  21. Very heavy volume raises the chance of a big move further, and the move that follows has leaned up, not down. Futures volume over 24 hours against its 30-day norm, read once a day on 8 coins since 2020 (median 0.98 times). At 2.5 times or more: 5.6% of days; for BTC 81 of 2,449 days in 40 separate episodes, about six a year. A big move started within 24 hours on 25% of days at 1.8 to 2.6 times, 32% at 2.6 to 4 times and 47% above 4 times (the "Big-move risk" panel uses these bands). The highest daily readings: BTC 5.9 times (14 March 2020), ETH 7.2 times (14 June 2022), SOL 31 times (10 November 2022, the FTX collapse). Direction: after 2.5 times or more, 58% of the big moves that followed went up (385); after price had already fallen more than 5% in 24 hours, 63% (109; 75% of 28 in 2024 to 2026). This disagrees with the liquidation-map reading in the same panel when many accounts are long (15% up, 53 moves); both samples are small, so when the two conflict, direction is unknown. Over the following 7 days, high-volume days after a fall with 3 or more accounts long per short were followed by a rise only 42% of the time (90 days, median -1.9%). Method: research/volume_extremes.py.
  22. After a heavy-volume new low, a big bounce is normal, but usually only after one more low, and shorting the bounce has no tested edge in a falling market. Events: price breaking its 20-day low with 24-hour volume at least 2 times normal (8 coins since 2020, 207 events, at least 7 days apart; 145 while price was below its 200-day average). In those falling-market cases, price made a lower low within 10 days 78% of the time (BTC and ETH: 92%), a median 11.5% further down, and then bounced a median 2.5 normal weekly swings (24%) from that lowest point, reaching its peak after a median 90 hours; it retraced a median 56% of the preceding 7-day fall. After the bounce peaked, price went below the pre-bounce low within 30 days only 38% of the time. Shorting with a stop and a target of one weekly swing each: at the fresh low, 47% won; after a bounce of half a swing, 46%; after one full swing, 53% (only 59% of cases bounced that far); at the 0.382 and 0.5 retracements, 46% each. In 2024 to 2026 none of these was better than a coin flip. In rising markets, shorting at the 0.5 retracement won 74% (39 cases), but that is not the falling-market case. Method: research/bounce_map.py.
  23. After the bounce from a heavy-volume low, the usual next step is a retest that holds just above the low (a slightly higher low), then a push above the bounce peak that does not last; a straight lower low comes first only about 1 time in 5. Same events as the bounce map, falling markets only, counting only bounces whose peak came at least 48 hours before the 10-day window ended (86 on 8 coins, 23 for BTC and ETH). Within 30 days after the bounce peak, price first went above that peak in 70% of cases (BTC and ETH: 17 of 23, 74%), first went below the pre-bounce low in 20% (BTC and ETH: 5 of 23), and did neither in 10%. The pullback after the peak typically came back close to the low: its lowest point was a median 4.0% above the bounce low (BTC and ETH: 2.4%, middle half between 1.6% below and 5.2% above). Breaking above the peak was usually brief: 30 days after the peak price was a median 5.8% below it (BTC and ETH: 5.0%), and in 34% of cases price went below the bounce low at some point in those 30 days. Not tested: whether a liquidation pile near the bounce target makes the bounce larger. Method: research/after_bounce.py.
  24. Being far below the first low does not mean the bottom is in: in real time there is no way to tell the last low, and another deeper low was the usual case. Same events as the bounce map, falling markets only (142 events on 8 coins, 36 for BTC and ETH). From the first hour price was already 10% below the event low (65 cases; BTC and ETH 18), price made another low more than 1% deeper within 10 days in 62% of cases (BTC and ETH: 72%), a median 2.8% further (BTC and ETH: 6.8%, middle half between 0.8% and 15.2%). At 5% below: 81% (BTC and ETH 74%), median 8.3% further. At 15% below: 76% (BTC and ETH 8 of 10), median 9.3% further. The bounce from the final low was still large, a median 22% for BTC and ETH. So the bounce map's "one more low" is a count after the fact, not a signal that the low is done. Method: research/already_down.py.
  25. The last low does leave traces when it happens: a lower wick, a volume climax, the crowd and open positions dropping fast. Three or four of these together made a new low the last one about half the time, against 14% normally. Still not proof, and the cut-offs are not yet confirmed on new data. Same falling-market events (142 on 8 coins), every hour that made a new low within 10 days of the event (932 hours); "last low" means no lower low in the next 10 days (132 hours, 14%). Each sign alone, chance the hour was the last low: hour closed in the upper half of its range after the new low 18% to 33% (closed near its low: 3%); 24-hour volume 4 times normal or more, or that hour's volume 10 times normal, about 26%; account ratio down 10% or more in 24 hours 22% (36% in 2023 to 2026); open interest down 5% or more in 24 hours 27%. Score = number of these four: 0 gave 4%, 1 gave 11%, 2 gave 18%, 3 gave 43% (91 hours, 58 events), 4 gave 71% (14 hours). Score 3 or more: 41% in 2020 to 2022 and 52% in 2023 to 2026; BTC and ETH alone 38% (11 of 29). The cut-offs were picked after looking at each sign separately, so the score must be rechecked on data from 2027. Method: research/final_low.py.
  26. Buying the pullback after a bounce is only a small edge in real time, and the last-low signs do not help there; principle 23 looks better than it is because it knows the bounce peak afterwards. Same falling-market events. Real-time rule, nothing known in advance: track the lowest price since the event and the highest price since that low; buy at the close of the first hour when the bounce has reached at least one normal weekly swing and price has fallen back into the lower half of it. Won ("won" as in principle 10: up one weekly swing before down one, within 30 days) 56% of 139 entries (2020 to 2022: 49%; 2023 to 2026: 60%; BTC and ETH: 53% of 36), against 51% for a random entry and 46% for buying a fresh low. The low broke within 10 days in 48% of these entries. Counting with hindsight (the known bounce peak, as principle 23 does) the same kind of entry won 71% and broke the low only 17% of the time, which shows how much hindsight flatters this pattern. The four last-low signs of principle 25 were rare at the pullback (27 of 614 hours had two or more) and those hours did worse, not better (52% against 72% with hindsight). Method: research/retest_strength.py.

Stops and targets at lows (BTC and ETH, entries since 2020, before fees and funding): with the account ratio under 2.5, a 10% stop with a 15% target won 58% for BTC (average +4.6% per trade, 49 trades) and 47% for ETH (+1.7%, 33 trades). With the ratio at 2.5 or more, every stop and target tested lost money on average. A tight 5% stop did worse than a 10% stop.

Tested and found not to work on their own: RSI oversold (a coin flip) · TD-9 count (a small one-week bounce at best, worse together with oversold RSI) · Fibonacci 0.618 (no better than 0.5 or 0.7) · the 200-week average as a floor (failed in 2022 and Nov 2026) · long wick, high volume or falling open interest at a low (no edge once measured from a real entry price) · how far price has already bounced from the low · how fast the crowd ratio is falling (the level matters, not the speed) · stock market, dollar and bond-yield trends · "liquidate the longs first, then up" · predicting a strong week from an ordinary day · negative funding on its own as a sign of a coming squeeze · ETF outflow streaks · exchange balance flows · Wikipedia attention (worked until 2022) · a big liquidation day as a bounce signal · combining many indicators (worse than the single best one on unseen data).

How these were tested: each idea was checked on past data with a fixed rule, in separate periods, and kept only if it pointed the same way in each. Lows and highs are 10-day extremes at least three normal weekly swings away from the 60-day high or low, on Binance futures for BTC, ETH, SOL, BNB, XRP, DOGE, ADA and LINK (ratios available since Dec 2021, BTC since Sep 2020). Entry is 24 hours after the low or high has held. A "win" means price then moved 2.25 normal weekly swings in the trade's favour before 1.5 against (for BTC roughly +15% before -10%) within 60 days. Events in the same week are not independent, so the real sample is about 100 to 200 separate weeks: these are odds, not certainties. Not financial advice.

The ratio is the number of Binance accounts holding a long position for every account holding a short one (Binance's "long/short account ratio", all accounts). It counts accounts, not money: a $100 account counts the same as a $10 million one. Every contract has a buyer and a seller, so when 70% of accounts are long, the 30% that are short hold on average more than twice as much each; a high ratio means many small accounts long against fewer, bigger accounts short. The chart pane "Long vs short" shows it per candle (purple), next to the same ratio for Binance's top 20% accounts by margin balance, measured by position size (yellow). The table splits every day since the data starts into five equal groups by the day's average ratio, then looks at the price 30 days later. The 30-day windows overlap, so neighbouring days are not independent results, and each group holds only around a dozen truly separate 30-day periods. Read it as how things have tended to go, not as a forecast. Source: Binance public archive (5-minute values, up to yesterday) and Coinalyze (today).

Stablecoin supply · dollars inside crypto

Range

Stablecoins are tokens pegged to one US dollar (USDT from Tether, USDC from Circle, and others). They are the cash traders use inside crypto. When someone deposits dollars with the issuer, new tokens are created and supply rises: fresh money has entered crypto and is ready to buy. When tokens are redeemed for dollars they are destroyed and supply falls: money has left. Top pane: supply in billions of US$ (all dollar stablecoins, USDT, USDC). Bottom pane: change in total supply over the previous 30 days (growing / shrinking). Hover the chart to see any day in the table. Rising supply does not guarantee buying: the new tokens can sit idle or go to other coins, but over the years falling supply has usually matched weak crypto markets. Source: DefiLlama, checked hourly; today's figure keeps updating until the day ends (UTC). Shown from 2019, because earlier records are incomplete.

Archive and foundation

Foundation 1: anything worth remembering goes into Principles.

When an idea comes up in analysis, it is tested on past data with a fixed rule first, then written into the Principles section with its numbers and the date, whether it worked or not. Ideas that failed stay listed, so they are not tried again by mistake. Nothing important should depend on anyone's memory or on a chat that can be lost. Each test's code is kept in the repository's research/ folder so the numbers can be reproduced.

Foundation 2: this site is built to keep running for good, with nobody tending it.

Data collection, the site build, the health check and the archive all run on their own schedules. A complete archive is published automatically on the 1st of every month, and also whenever the working repository passes 600 MB: every file (all data, the code, the research and the notes) plus the whole change history. Archives are stored as GitHub releases, which GitHub does not limit in total size, so the archive itself cannot fill up. All archives: github.com/wahyunugi/liqmap-data/releases.

ArchiveDownload

What is not solved yet (16 Nov 2026): archiving protects the data but does not make the working repository smaller, because every hourly update stays in its history. GitHub recommends staying under 1 GB and strongly under 5 GB; these are recommendations, not an automatic shutdown. The lasting fix is to keep the hourly data outside the repository's history, which is planned but not done. What can still stop the site, whatever the storage: GitHub changing its free service, a data source closing or changing how it is read (Binance archive, Coinalyze, Farside, DefiLlama, Hyperliquid), or the Coinalyze access key expiring. The health check emails the owner when data stops arriving.

What you are looking at

Heatmap (behind the candles): estimated liquidation levels at each moment, from the repo's model v6, a replica of CoinGlass Model 1. Each column shows the levels created during the window before it that price has not touched yet. Brighter = more USD.

Window (how long a level counts): 24 hours up to 1D candles, 7 days on 1W, 30 days on 1M. Only the 24-hour window is checked against CoinGlass; 7 and 30 days use the same rules with levels kept longer. When zoomed out, neighbouring price boxes are added together so each row stays readable.

1D, 1W, 1M go back to 2020: candles and open interest from the Binance public archive (data.binance.vision), joined with this repo's data. The heatmap is calculated with the same model and checked equal to the repo's Python model.

Fibonacci (dashed lines, switch in the toolbar): retracement levels of the biggest swing among the candles you are looking at, from the lowest low to the highest high. If the low came first, the lines show how much of that rise has been given back (0 = the high, 1 = the low); if the high came first, how much of the fall has been recovered (0 = the low, 1 = the high). 0.618 means 61.8% of the swing. The levels use a log scale (equal percentage steps) and follow the view: zoom or scroll to pick another swing. The legend shows which swing is used. Traders watch these levels, but in this site's tests 0.618 held no better than 0.5 or 0.7 (see Principles), so treat them as reference lines, not signals.

Liquidation map (bars on the right): the same levels for the candle under your cursor, or the latest candle, as a side profile. blue = long positions that get liquidated if price falls to that level; orange = short positions liquidated if price rises to it. Longer bar = more USD.

Lower panes

RSI 14: the Relative Strength Index (J. Welles Wilder, 1978) of the candles shown, over 14 candles: 70 or more means strong recent buying ("overbought"), 30 or less strong recent selling ("oversold"). Arrows and lines mark regular divergences, found with the same rules as TradingView's built-in "RSI Divergence Indicator": an RSI turning point (lower or higher than the 5 candles on each side) is compared with the previous one 5 to 60 candles earlier. Bullish: price makes a lower low but RSI a higher low, so the selling is losing force. Bearish: price makes a higher high but RSI a lower high. Solid line on the RSI pane, dashed line on the price. A turning point is only known 5 candles later, so each divergence appears 5 candles after the low or high. A divergence shows momentum fading, not that the trend has turned: in a strong trend several can appear in a row before price reverses, and some never lead to one.

Actual liquidations: recorded on Binance (Coinalyze feed). Shorts liquidated plot upward, longs liquidated downward. Undercounts the real total, because Binance publishes only the largest liquidation per coin per second.

Open interest: the total size of futures positions still open on Binance, in coins (each contract has a buyer and a seller and is counted once). Rising = new positions opening; falling = positions closing or being liquidated. The heatmap is built from its increases.

Volume: coins traded per candle.

Futures vs spot · funding: the line is how far Binance's perpetual futures price sits above (+) or below (−) Binance's spot price, in percent, averaged over the candle. When spot buyers lead a move up, futures lag and the gap falls; when futures traders lead, the gap rises. The bars are the funding rate, averaged over Binance, Bybit and OKX, in percent per 8-hour period: yellow (positive) = longs pay shorts, so longs are the crowded side; purple (negative) = shorts pay longs. Funding: hourly for 60 days, daily since 2020 (Coinalyze).

Other exchanges: the actual-liquidations and open-interest labels add Bybit and OKX perpetual futures (Coinalyze). The spot pane adds Kraken and Gemini to the US group, and Bybit, OKX, Bithumb and Upbit (Korea) to the non-US group. Coinalyze provides Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini, Binance, Bybit and Bithumb (5-minute for about a week, daily since 2020); OKX comes from OKX's own 5-minute buy/sell statistics and Upbit is counted trade by trade, both from 2 Nov 2026. CME daily volume and open interest could not be collected: CME's website refuses requests from the GitHub servers that run the updates.

Long vs short: Binance accounts holding a long for every account holding a short (purple, all accounts), and the long/short ratio of Binance's top 20% accounts by margin balance measured by position size (yellow). 1.0 = even. See "Crowd positioning" below for what high values have meant historically.

Spot net buying: who is pushing the spot price. For each candle, coins bought by market orders minus coins sold by market orders (the side that moves the price), on Coinbase (green: US buyers and sellers) and Binance spot (yellow: non-US). The lines are running totals that both start at zero where both venues have data (Coinbase is only counted from 1 Nov 2026), so they cover the same period: rising = buyers more aggressive, falling = sellers more aggressive. The label also shows the same figure for Binance perpetual futures, which usually trade several times more. Both lines start where every venue shown has data. On 1D/1W/1M the lines use daily data and leave out OKX and Upbit, which have no daily history.

Coinbase premium: how much higher (above zero) or lower (below zero) bitcoin or ether trades on Coinbase, where US buyers and most US institutions trade, than on Binance spot, in percent, averaged over the candle. Binance prices are in USDT, so they are converted to dollars with Coinbase's USDT price first; otherwise the gap mostly shows USDT trading slightly off $1. Above zero = US buyers are paying up; below zero = US sellers are more eager. It is a reading of behaviour, not a record of who traded. Data: 5-minute values for the last 60 days, daily averages since June 2021 on 1D/1W/1M.

Per candle, on every interval: volume and actual liquidations are totals over the candle; open interest is the value at the candle's close; the heatmap column and the liquidation map show the levels still open at the candle's close (not a sum over the candle).

Data and limits

The heatmap shows where new money entered, not where liquidations are guaranteed. It assumes 25x/50x/100x leverage and isolated margin. Times are WIB (UTC+7); candle buckets follow Binance (UTC).
